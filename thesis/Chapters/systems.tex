\noindent
Chapter~\ref{ch:literature} ended with a construct and a set of propositions
that the data may reject. It did not say how the parts of that construct act on
one another over time, or how the chain of files, models and decisions that
will test it can fail. This chapter asks what changes when both are treated as
systems: what the boundary of each is, which parts move together, where a
change does most good, and where an error enters and how far it travels. The
question matters before any equation is written, because the formalism of
Chapter~\ref{ch:methods} and the realisation model of Chapter~\ref{chap:crf}
each make choices that only a systems view can justify. The choice of a
multiplicative realisation function is a claim about how the domains interact.
The choice to freeze the measurement model before any outcome is read is a
claim about where errors in an analysis come from. Neither claim is obvious, and
this chapter states the reasoning behind both.

\section{Two Systems}
\label{sec:sys-intro}

The thesis deals with two systems, and it helps to keep them apart from the
start.

The first is the system being measured. A person's health capacity in later
life is produced by biomedical, social and systemic parts that act on one
another. Chronic disease limits what a body can do; limited function reduces
how often a person leaves the house; fewer contacts mean less help when help
is needed; and the cost of care, the layout of a home and the distance to a
clinic shape all of these. No single part holds the capacity. It belongs to
the arrangement. That is the sense in which Chapter~\ref{ch:literature}
called the construct relational, and it is the first reason a systems view is
needed: a property that belongs to an arrangement cannot be read from any one
of its parts.

The second is the system doing the measuring. Between the NHATS public-use
files and a number printed in Chapter~\ref{ch:results1} sit a layout file, a
build script, a set of checks, an imputation model, a joint factor model, a
scoring rule, survival models, a validation procedure and a step that writes
tables into the thesis. Each stage takes the output of the one before it and
trusts it. An error made early, such as reading a response code the wrong way
round, does not stay where it was made. It passes through every later stage
and arrives in the results looking like a finding. Appendix~\ref{app:datachecks}
records five errors of that kind that were caught before any analysis was run.
The second reason for a systems view is that a chain of trusting stages needs
to be designed so that errors are caught, and the design has to be stated
before it can be checked.

Systems thinking in health has grown from a wish to explain why interventions
that work in trials so often disappoint in practice. \citet{peters_2014}
argues that health problems sit inside systems with many actors, feedback and
delays, and that methods which isolate a single cause miss the behaviour that
matters. \citet{diez_roux_2011} makes the same case for health disparities:
the persistence of unequal health across generations is a property of feedback
among individual, social and institutional processes, and regression on
independent risk factors cannot represent it. \citet{rutter_2017} asks public
health to judge evidence by how an intervention changes the system it enters,
not by whether it changes a single outcome in isolation. Each of these papers
sets a standard that a capability construct should meet: it should say how
its parts depend on one another and what follows when one of them moves.

The ideas themselves are older. \citet{vonbertalanffy_1968} proposed a general
theory of open systems, whose behaviour depends on exchange with an
environment and whose properties belong to the whole rather than to any part.
\citet{ashby_1956} gave the control of such systems a precise form: a
regulator can hold a system steady only if it can respond to as many kinds of
disturbance as the system can suffer. \citet{meadows_2008} set out the working
vocabulary used here, of stocks, flows, delays and feedback loops, in a form
that does not need differential equations to be useful. The thesis borrows
from both traditions. From the first it takes the idea that health capacity is
a property of an arrangement. From the second it takes the habit of asking,
for any measuring system, what disturbances it can detect and what it is blind
to.

The chapter has two halves that mirror the two systems.
Sections~\ref{sec:sys-boundary} to~\ref{sec:sys-resilience} treat the system
being measured. They fix its boundary, describe capacity as a stock and
realised health as a flow, set out the feedback among the three domains, ask
where a change in that system does most good, and consider what the construct
can say about resilience. Sections~\ref{sec:sys-pipeline}
to~\ref{sec:sys-control} treat the measuring system. They describe the
pipeline as an engineered system with requirements and checks, list its
failure modes with the controls against each, and set out who acts on the
score and what they learn from acting. Section~\ref{sec:sys-limits} states
what systems thinking cannot do for this thesis, and
Section~\ref{sec:sys-close} says what each later chapter takes from this one.

A caution belongs at the start. Nothing in this chapter is evidence for the
construct. The diagrams and loop structures below organise questions and
design checks; they do not show that the three domains exist, that the
indicators measure them, or that the score predicts anything. Those questions
belong to the data, and the chapter's last substantive section returns to this
point.

\section{Boundary, Components and Interfaces}
\label{sec:sys-boundary}

The opening section left a question that every systems analysis must answer
first: where does the system end? A boundary decides what the analysis
explains and what it takes as given. Draw it too tight and the causes of the
behaviour lie outside it; draw it too wide and nothing can be measured.
\citet{checkland_1981} called these choices boundary judgements and insisted
that they be stated, since they are made by the analyst and not found in the
world. This section states the judgements made here.

\paragraph{Four levels.} Health capacity in later life can be described at
four levels. The first is the person: their body, their mind and the
functions these support. The second is the household: who else lives there,
what income enters it, what the home is like. The third is the set of
services the person can reach: clinics, hospitals, transport and paid or
unpaid help. The fourth is the policy environment: the rules of Medicare and
Medicaid, the price of care, the design of housing and the law. Each level
acts on the ones inside it, and in the long run the inner levels act back, as
when the needs of an ageing population shape the services built for it.

\paragraph{What is endogenous and what is given.} A part of a system is
endogenous when the analysis explains it from other parts of the system, and
given (or exogenous) when the analysis takes it as fixed from outside. The
thesis treats the first two levels and part of the third as inside the
system. A person's chronic
conditions, function, cognition and mood make up Biomedical Potential. Their
participation in activities, the size of their network and their means of
staying in contact make up Social Modifiers. Their income, education, the
barriers in their home and the barriers they meet in reaching care make up
Systemic Burdens. These eleven indicators are the observed face of the three
domains, and the domains can change one another. The fourth level, and the
supply side of the third, are taken as given. The thesis does not model how a
clinic decides its opening hours, how Medicare sets its rules, or how a city
builds its transport. These shape Systemic Burdens, but the thesis observes
only their effect on the person, through indicators such as whether the person
could reach care when they needed it.

The cost of this choice should be plain. A policy that changes the fourth
level, such as a new rule on home adaptations, enters the system only through
the indicators it moves. If it moves something the indicators do not record,
the construct cannot see it. A wider boundary would avoid that blindness but
would need data on services and policies that NHATS does not hold and that no
individual-level survey could hold in full. The narrower boundary was chosen
because it can be measured, and its blindness is stated rather than hidden.

\paragraph{The observation interface.} Every system has a point at which the
analyst sees it, and that point shapes what can be known. Here it is the
NHATS interview: a yearly conversation with a sample person or a proxy,
coded into public-use files. The interface has properties that matter for the
argument. It sees the person once a year, so anything that happens between
interviews is either reported later or missed. It sees through a proxy for
people who cannot answer for themselves, and some items are not asked of
proxies at all, which means the people with the lowest capacity are seen
least clearly (Appendix~\ref{app:datachecks}). It records the month of each
interview and of each final case status, but not the exact date of death. An
engineer would say the sensor has a sampling interval of a year, a known blind
spot for the most impaired, and a timing resolution of a month. Those
properties limit every later claim, and Chapter~\ref{ch:methods} sets the
analysis to fit within them.

\paragraph{Components.} The three domains are the components of the measured
system. They are defined by what they contribute rather than by where they
sit. Biomedical Potential is what the body and mind make possible. Social
Modifiers are the conditions that let a person turn that possibility into
activity, and Systemic Burdens are the conditions that stand in the way. The
same fact about the world can touch more than one domain: low income affects
diet, which affects disease, and also affects whether a person can pay for
transport to a clinic. The thesis does not assume the domains are
independent. It assumes they are distinct, which is a weaker claim and one
that the measurement model of Chapter~\ref{ch:methods} tests by asking whether
a correlated three-domain structure fits better than a single factor.

\paragraph{The interface that couples the components.} The three domains
have to be combined into one statement of how much a person can do, and the
rule that combines them decides whether strength in one domain can make up for
weakness in another. The expression below is that rule. It is given here only
to show its shape; Chapter~\ref{chap:crf} derives it. The realisation
function of Chapter~\ref{chap:crf},
\[
H(P,S,R)=P\,S^{\alpha}R^{1-\alpha},\qquad R=1-B,
\]
is the interface between the components and the property the thesis cares
about. It states how biomedical potential $P$, social enablement $S$ and
structural freedom $R$ combine into realised capability $H$. In engineering
terms it is the specification of how the subsystems connect, and like any
interface it constrains what can happen on either side. Because it is
multiplicative, no component can make up for the absence of another: if $S$
or $R$ is zero, $H$ is zero whatever the value of $P$. This is the
non-compensation axiom of Chapter~\ref{chap:crf}, and in systems language it
says that the components are coupled in series rather than in parallel. A
series arrangement fails when any link fails; a parallel one keeps working
while any link holds. Chapter~\ref{ch:literature} argued from theory that
health capability behaves in series, and Section~\ref{sec:sys-leverage} draws
out what that implies for policy.

\paragraph{Emergence.} One consequence of the boundary and the interface
should be stated now, because it governs how the score may be read. Realised
capability is a property of the whole that no domain possesses alone. Two
people with the same biomedical potential can have very different realised
capability, and two people with the same realised capability can reach it by
very different routes. The score therefore cannot be read backwards to say
which domain is responsible for a low value; that requires the domain profile
itself, which is why the thesis reports the profile alongside any summary
score. Reading a single number as if it located the problem is the first
misuse that Section~\ref{sec:sys-control} guards against.

\paragraph{Boundary of the measuring system.} The measuring system has a
boundary too. It begins at the NHATS public-use files as released and ends at
the tables and figures written into this thesis. What happens before the
files, such as how NHATS fields its survey, and what happens after the
thesis, such as how anyone might use the score, lie outside. The first is
taken as given and checked only where NHATS publishes the documents to check
it against. The second is outside the thesis's control but not outside its
responsibility, which is why Section~\ref{sec:sys-control} treats the use of
the score as part of the design.

With the boundary fixed, the next question is how the system inside it
changes over time.

\section{Stocks, Flows and Delays}
\label{sec:sys-stocks}

A boundary says what is inside the system. It does not say how the inside
changes. The basic distinction in system dynamics is between a stock, which is
an amount that accumulates, and a flow, which is a rate that adds to or drains
it \citep{forrester_1961,sterman_2000}. The level of a stock at any moment
reflects the whole history of its flows, which is why stocks give a system memory and
why they change slowly even when the flows change fast. This section reads
the construct in those terms.

\paragraph{Capacity as a stock.} Biomedical Potential behaves like a stock. It
is built over a lifetime by growth, education, nutrition and care, and it is
drawn down by disease, injury and age. At any interview it reflects all that
came before. A person does not lose function overnight because of a bad
month, and does not regain it overnight because of a good one. Social
Modifiers and Systemic Burdens are partly stocks as well: a network of
friends, savings, a paid-off home and years of schooling are all
accumulations. Other parts are closer to flows, such as monthly income or the
number of times in a month a person goes out.

\paragraph{Realised health as a flow.} Realised capability, $H$, is better
read as a flow. It is what the stock of capacity yields, per unit of time, in
the conditions the person lives in. On the natural scale of
Chapter~\ref{chap:crf}, where $P=L\cdot Q$ is measured in quality-adjusted
life-years, $H$ is a number of good years realised; but the same reading holds
on the unit scale, where $H$ is the share of the calibration population's
range that a person reaches. The distinction matters because a flow can fall
while the stock behind it is intact. A person whose bus route is cut may stop
attending a day centre. Their realised capability falls at once, but their
biomedical potential has not changed. If the route is restored soon, the flow
recovers. If it is not, the lost activity begins in time to draw down the
stock as well, and the person may not regain what they had.

\paragraph{Inflows and outflows.} The inflows to the biomedical stock include
treatment, rehabilitation, exercise and the repair the body carries out by
itself. The outflows include the progression of chronic disease, acute events
such as a fall or a stroke, and the slow losses of age. Social and systemic
stocks have their own flows: a network gains members through activity and
loses them through death, moves and withdrawal; savings rise with income and
fall with spending on care. The three domains share flows, which is the source
of the feedback set out in Section~\ref{sec:sys-loops}. Spending on care is an
outflow from savings and an inflow to biomedical capacity at once.

Figure~\ref{fig:sys-stocks} draws the biomedical part of this account in the
standard notation of system dynamics. Capacity is the stock, drawn as a box.
Three inflows fill it and two outflows drain it, each drawn as a double-line
pipe with a valve that sets its rate. Realised health leaves the stock towards
daily life through a further valve. The other two domains appear as the hands
on the valves, not as stocks in the drawing: systemic burden narrows the
treatment valve, because high costs and barriers delay or prevent care, and
social enablement and structural freedom, $R=1-B$, set how much of the
capacity reaches daily life. The figure therefore shows in one place the
distinction the realisation function rests on: $P$ is how much is in the
stock, while $S$ and $R$ decide how much of it flows out as realised
capability.

A haematologist works with a stock of this kind every day. The plasma level of
a clotting factor in a person with haemophilia is a stock: an infusion of
factor concentrate is an inflow, clearance from the circulation is an outflow,
and the trough level before the next dose reflects the balance of the two over
the dosing interval. A single factor assay reads the level of that stock at
one moment, as a baseline H-potential score reads the level of capacity at
one interview. The parallel holds for the structure of the account. It breaks
down in two places. A factor level is measured directly by an assay against a
reference standard, while capacity is estimated from several indicators that
each reflect it imperfectly. And the time scale differs: a factor level moves
over hours and days, while capacity moves over months and years.

\paragraph{Delays.} Between a change in one part of the system and its effect
on another there is usually a delay, and delays shape what a single
measurement can show. Three are central here. The first is the delay in
diagnosis: a disease may draw down capacity for years before it is named and
treated, so the inflow of treatment starts late. The second is the delay in
access: once a need is known, reaching the service that meets it takes time,
and the delay is longer where Systemic Burdens are high. The third is the
delay between a change in income or support and its effect on health, which
may run to years. \citet{sterman_2000} shows that delays in a feedback loop
are a common cause of overshoot and oscillation, because the corrective action
continues after the need for it has passed, or begins after the damage is
done.

Delays bear on the design of the thesis in two ways. First, they mean that a
baseline score taken at one interview reflects the stocks at that moment,
including the effects of past flows that may already have changed. A person
whose care access has just improved will not yet show the benefit. Second,
they set the time scale of the outcomes. Mortality and hospitalisation over
the follow-up rounds are the result of flows acting on stocks over years, and
a baseline score can predict them only to the extent that the stocks it
records persist. Chapter~\ref{ch:results2} tests that persistence indirectly,
by asking whether the baseline score predicts outcomes years later.

\paragraph{The static reading and the dynamic extension.} The empirical
construct is a reading of the stocks at one time. Appendix~\ref{app:dynamical}
sets out what a reading over time would look like. It
introduces a state variable $X(t)$ behind the biomedical term, whose rate of
change rises with treatment and repair and falls with inhibition and
clearance, and which settles at a level where these balance. In stock-and-flow
terms, $X(t)$ is a stock, the terms of its differential equation are the
flows, and the level at which it settles is the equilibrium at which inflow
equals outflow. The appendix then adds noise, because two people on the same
regimen do not follow the same path, and finally follows a whole cohort as a
density that moves and spreads. The static score of the empirical chapters is
the special case in which time does not appear. The appendix is a proposed
direction and is not used in the validation, but it shows that the
stock-and-flow reading is more than a figure of speech: the construct can be
given dynamics without changing its definition.

\paragraph{Why stocks change slowly, and why it matters here.}
\citet{meadows_2008} notes that the slowness of stocks is what lets a system
be managed at all: a stock buffers the flows and gives time to act. For this
thesis the same slowness has a second consequence. It is the reason a single
baseline measurement can carry information about outcomes years later. If
capacity were a flow that changed month to month, a reading at one interview
would say little about the next five years. Because it is a stock, the reading
persists. The prognostic analysis of Chapter~\ref{ch:results2} rests on that
persistence without stating it, and this section states it.

The stocks and flows describe what accumulates. They do not yet say why some
changes feed on themselves while others correct themselves. That is the
subject of the next section.

\section{Feedback Among the Three Domains}
\label{sec:sys-loops}

Stocks and flows describe what accumulates. Feedback describes why some
changes grow and others fade. A feedback loop is a closed chain of causes in
which a change in one part returns, through the others, to act on itself.
Each link in such a chain has a polarity. A link is positive when a rise in
the cause moves the effect in the same direction, and negative when it moves
the effect in the opposite direction. The polarity of the loop is the product
of the polarities of its links: a loop with no negative links, or an even
number of them, is reinforcing and amplifies any change; a loop with an odd
number of negative links is balancing and works against change, pulling the
system towards some level \citep{sterman_2000,meadows_2008}. Reinforcing loops
produce growth and collapse. Balancing loops produce stability, and also the
resistance that frustrates many policies.

Figure~\ref{fig:sys-loops} sets out the feedback among the three domains that
the theory of Chapter~\ref{ch:literature} implies. It is drawn in general terms
from that theory and from the boundary of
Section~\ref{sec:sys-boundary}. No link in it is estimated, and no strength is
attached to any link. Its purpose is to make the causal claims behind the
construct explicit enough to be questioned.

not a measured lag. The three links into realised capability $H$ are the
realisation function of Chapter~\ref{chap:crf} and form no loop, because the
thesis measures $H$ at one time.}
\label{fig:sys-loops}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{R1: capacity and participation.} The upper loop in
Figure~\ref{fig:sys-loops} links Biomedical Potential and social enablement.
A person with more capacity can take part in more: they go out, see others,
volunteer and keep up the contacts that make up a network. Participation in
turn maintains capacity, through physical activity, mental stimulation and the
practical help that a network provides when an illness strikes. Both links are
positive, so the loop is reinforcing. Run downwards, it describes a familiar
pattern in later life: an illness cuts activity, the loss of activity weakens
the body and the network, and the person comes out of the illness with less
than they had before it. Run upwards, the same loop describes a person whose
activity keeps them able to remain active. The return link is drawn dashed
because the effect of participation on capacity builds over months and years,
while the effect of capacity on participation can be felt within weeks.

\paragraph{R2: capacity and burden.} The lower loop links Biomedical Potential
and Systemic Burdens. A fall in capacity raises burden: care costs rise, a
home that was adequate becomes a barrier, and a trip to a clinic that was
easy becomes hard. The link is negative, because more capacity means less
burden. Burden in turn draws down capacity: high costs lead people to delay or
forgo care, and barriers in the home cut activity. That link is also negative.
Two negative links make a reinforcing loop. It is the loop through which
illness and hardship feed one another, and it is drawn with a dashed return
link because the effect of burden on capacity, like the effect of
participation, takes time to show.

\paragraph{B1: capacity and care.} The left-hand loop is the one a health
system is built to supply. A fall in capacity raises the need for care, and
more care is sought and received; the link from capacity to care received is
negative. Care restores capacity, or slows its loss; that link is positive.
One negative link makes a balancing loop. It works to hold capacity near a
level, and it is the reason most people recover from most illnesses. Its
return link is dashed because the effect of care runs through the delays of
Section~\ref{sec:sys-stocks}: a need must be noticed, a diagnosis made and a
service reached before treatment can act.

\paragraph{The coupling that matters most.} The link from Systemic Burdens to
care received is the one that ties the loops together. It is negative: the
higher the burden, the less care a given need produces. Its effect is to
weaken B1 exactly where R2 is strongest. A person with high burden is more
exposed to the reinforcing loop that drives decline, and has a weaker
balancing loop to stop it. This is a mechanism by which disadvantages
compound rather than add, and it gives a dynamic reason to expect the positive
cross-partials of the realisation function that Chapter~\ref{chap:crf}
derives from axioms. The two arguments are consistent; neither proves the
other. The cross-partials are a property of a static function, and the loops
are a hypothesis about how the world moves.

\paragraph{Which loop dominates.} A system with both kinds of loop can behave
in more than one way, depending on which loop is stronger at the time. When a
person has ample capacity, the balancing loop is likely to dominate. A shock is
absorbed, care restores what was lost, and the person returns near their
earlier level. When capacity is low and burden high, the reinforcing loops may
dominate, so that each loss makes the next more likely. The change between
these regimes need not be gradual. Near the level at which the reinforcing
loops overtake the balancing one, a small further loss can tip the system
from recovery into decline. This is the sense in which the system is
nonlinear: the response to a shock depends on the state in which the shock
arrives as well as on its size. Section~\ref{sec:sys-resilience} returns to
what such a threshold would mean for measurement.

\paragraph{What the thesis can test, and what it cannot.} The loops are
hypotheses. The empirical design of this thesis cannot test them directly, and
it is better to say so here than to let the figure imply otherwise. The
measurement model uses indicators from a single round. Correlations among the
three domains at one time are what feedback would leave behind, but they are
also what a common cause, such as a lifetime of poverty acting on all three
domains at once, would leave behind. A cross-section cannot tell the two
apart. The prognostic analysis tests a consequence of the loops, that a
baseline reading of the stocks predicts later outcomes, but a prediction can
succeed for reasons other than the ones drawn here. A direct test would need
repeated measurement of all three domains and a model of how each round's
values depend on the last. NHATS collects the repeated rounds; the thesis does
not model them, and Appendix~\ref{app:dynamical} sets out the form such a
model would take. The figure therefore serves two purposes in this thesis. It
states the causal reasoning that justifies the realisation function, and it
names the longitudinal study that would be needed to test that reasoning.

The loops show how the system moves. The next question is where a change
would move it most.

\section{Leverage Points}
\label{sec:sys-leverage}

If feedback decides how the system responds to a change, the choice of where
to make the change is not neutral. \citet{meadows_1999} ordered the places to
intervene in a system by how much each can alter its behaviour. At the bottom
of her list are numbers: the parameters, subsidies and standards that absorb
most of the attention of policy. Above them come the sizes of buffers, the
physical structure of stocks and flows, the length of delays, the strength of
balancing loops and the gain of reinforcing ones. Higher still come the flows
of information, the rules of the system, its power to change its own
structure, its goals, and at the top the set of shared ideas from which the
system arises. Her claim is that the higher places are harder to reach but
change more, and that people often push the lower ones hard while ignoring the
higher.

\paragraph{The list mapped to the domains.} Each of these places has a
counterpart in the system of Figure~\ref{fig:sys-loops}. The lowest, the
numbers, are mostly found in Systemic Burdens: the size of a co-payment, the
level of a benefit, the income threshold for a programme. Buffers are the
stocks that absorb shocks: savings on the systemic side, and on the biomedical
side the reserve that lets a person lose some function without losing
independence. The physical structure of stocks and flows is the built
environment: the stock of accessible housing, the routes of public transport,
the location of clinics. These are slow and costly to change, which is why
they shape Systemic Burdens for decades. Delays sit on the dashed links of the
figure; shortening the delay between need and care strengthens B1 without
changing anything else. The gain of the reinforcing loops can be cut at
particular links: support that keeps a person taking part in activities after
an acute illness slows R1, and protection against high care costs slows R2.

Information flows sit higher on the list, and here the construct itself
becomes an intervention. A score that reports a person's domain profile adds
a flow of information that did not exist before: it tells a clinician or a
programme manager which domain is binding. \citet{meadows_1999} placed new
information flows above most changes of parameters, because people act
differently when they can see what they could not see before. The same
argument is the reason Section~\ref{sec:sys-control} treats the use of the
score as part of the design. Rules sit above information: who is eligible for
what, and on what evidence. The governance rule of Chapter~\ref{ch:methods},
that the score may not be the sole basis for automated denial, reimbursement
or triage, is a rule of exactly this kind. At the top of the list, the goals
of the system and the ideas behind them, sits the argument of
Chapter~\ref{ch:literature}: that a health system might aim at realised
capability rather than at the absence of disease alone. The thesis does not
claim to move that level. It offers a way of measuring progress if someone
else does.

\paragraph{Why the weakest domain binds.} The realisation function adds a
second, more formal, answer to the question of where to act. The two
expressions below give the gain in realised capability from a small rise in
each contextual domain, with everything else held where it is. A planner
choosing between a programme that raises social enablement and one that
lowers systemic burden would compare exactly these two quantities. For the
primary form, the gradients of Chapter~\ref{chap:crf} are
\[
\frac{\partial H}{\partial S}=\alpha\,\frac{H}{S},
\qquad
\frac{\partial H}{\partial R}=(1-\alpha)\,\frac{H}{R}.
\]
The gain from one more unit of a contextual domain is inversely proportional
to that domain's current level. The ratio of the two gains is
\[
\frac{\partial H/\partial S}{\partial H/\partial R}
=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\cdot\frac{R}{S}.
\]
With equal weights, $\alpha=1/2$, the ratio is simply $R/S$. If a person's
structural freedom is three times their social enablement, a unit gain in
social enablement raises realised capability three times as much as a unit
gain in structural freedom. The domain that is lowest relative to its weight
is the one where a unit of change buys most. Two further features push the
same way. Each domain is bounded above by $1$, so a domain that is already
high has little room left to rise. And as either contextual domain falls
towards zero, realised capability falls towards zero whatever the other
domains hold. In the wider family of Chapter~\ref{chap:crf}, a substitution
parameter $\rho<0$ makes the contextual mean move closer to the smaller of its
two arguments as $\rho$ falls, so the weakest domain binds harder still.

The elasticities tell the same story from another angle. They are fixed:
$1$ for $P$, $\alpha$ for $S$ and $1-\alpha$ for $R$. A ten per cent rise in
any one domain therefore yields the same proportional rise in realised
capability whatever that domain's level. But ten per cent of a low value is a
small absolute change, and ten per cent of a high value may be impossible.
The policy question is usually about absolute changes bought with a budget,
and on that question the gradients, not the elasticities, are the guide.

\paragraph{What this means for where policy acts.} Three consequences follow,
each with a limit. First, improving the strongest domain buys little.
A programme that adds clinical treatment for a person whose binding constraint
is that they cannot reach the clinic will show a small gain in realised
capability, because the treatment raises a domain that was not binding. Under
an additive index the same programme would score well, since an additive
index lets strength in one domain cover weakness in another; that is the
substitution the non-compensation axiom forbids. Second, the binding domain
differs from person to person, so a policy aimed at one domain will help some
people much and others little. A population average of the gradient hides
this. The equity-sensitive population measure and the subgroup reporting of
Chapter~\ref{chap:crf} exist to keep it visible. Third, the gradient ranks
model-implied gains per unit of domain, not per unit of money and not as
causal effects. The budget-constrained calculation of Chapter~\ref{chap:crf}
divides each gain by its cost, and the ranking can reverse when the cheap
domain to improve is not the binding one. Even then, it needs response
functions that say how much a given programme moves a domain, and those are
causal quantities this thesis does not estimate. The evidence vector of that
chapter keeps the domain changes, the health gain, the costs, the budget
impact, the distribution and the legal constraints as separate coordinates,
so that a decision can weigh them openly.

The feedback of Section~\ref{sec:sys-loops} adds one more caution. The
gradient is a static quantity. An intervention at the binding domain may also
weaken a reinforcing loop, as when restored transport lets a person resume the
activity that maintains their capacity. Its long-run effect could then exceed
what the gradient suggests. The reverse is also possible: a balancing loop
elsewhere in the system may absorb part of the change. The static model cannot
say which. It can say where the first effect of a change is largest, and the
thesis claims no more than that.

Knowing where to push says nothing about what happens when the system is
pushed by events no one chose. That is the subject of the next section.

\section{Reserve, Robustness and Resilience}
\label{sec:sys-resilience}

The last section asked where a deliberate change does most good. Most changes
in later life are not deliberate: a fall, an infection, a bereavement or the
loss of a carer arrives unasked. The question this section takes up is what
the construct can say about how a person's system responds to such a shock.
Three related ideas are needed, and they are often confused.

\paragraph{Reserve.} Reserve is the amount by which a stock exceeds the level
at which function fails. A person with ample reserve can lose a good deal of
capacity before they lose independence; a person with little reserve cannot.
Reserve is a property of the stock at one time, and so it is the property
closest to what a single baseline reading measures.

\paragraph{Resistance to perturbation.} The second property in the title of
this section is the one \citet{kitano_2004} studies in biological systems: the
ability of a system to maintain its functions against perturbations. He traces it to a small number of design features:
feedback control, redundancy, so that another mechanism takes over when one
fails, modularity, so that damage stays local, and decoupling, so that
variation at one level does not pass to the next. Kitano also notes that a
system tuned to resist the perturbations it commonly meets may be fragile to
those it rarely meets. In the terms of this chapter, the balancing loop B1 is
a feedback control, and the multiplicative interface of
Section~\ref{sec:sys-boundary} is the opposite of redundancy: a series
arrangement in which a shock to any domain passes straight to realised
capability.

\paragraph{Resilience.} \citet{holling_1973} separated two properties that
the word stability had blurred. Stability, in his sense, is how quickly a
system returns to equilibrium after a disturbance. Resilience is how large a
disturbance the system can absorb before it moves to a different state
altogether, one from which it does not return. A system can be stable but not
resilient, returning quickly from small shocks and failing under a large one.
\citet{whitson_2016} reviewed how the idea has been applied to older adults
and described physical resilience as the ability to resist or recover from
functional decline after a health stressor. They treat it as an emerging
construct, defined by the response to a stressor rather than by a state
measured in its absence.

\paragraph{Early warning.} If a system can move to a different state, it is
natural to ask whether the move can be seen coming. \citet{scheffer_2009}
showed that many systems approaching a critical transition share generic
signs: they recover more slowly from small disturbances, and their
fluctuations grow larger and more strongly correlated from one observation to
the next. \citet{olde_rikkert_2016} proposed that slower recovery could serve
as a general marker of risk for acute transitions in chronic disease. The
signal in both cases is dynamic. It lies in how the system responds over time,
not in its level at one moment.

\paragraph{What H-potential reads.} Against these definitions the position of
the construct is clear. H-potential is a static reading of a dynamic property.
It records the level of the stocks at one interview: how much capacity a
person has, how enabling their circumstances are, how heavy their burdens.
That is closest to reserve. It does not record how fast the person returns
after a shock, which is stability in Holling's sense, or how large a shock
they can take, which is resilience. Two people with the same score could
differ in both, if the loops of Figure~\ref{fig:sys-loops} are of different
strength in their lives.

The construct can claim three things. It can claim to measure something
related to reserve, to the extent that its domains capture the stocks. It can
claim that, if reserve and resilience are related, a higher score should go
with better outcomes after shocks; the prognostic analysis of
Chapter~\ref{ch:results2} tests a weaker form of this, through death and
hospitalisation over follow-up. And it can claim that, because the
realisation function is multiplicative, a low value in any one domain marks a
person whose realised capability is exposed to shocks in that domain.

It cannot claim to measure resilience, and it cannot claim to warn of a
transition. Both require repeated observation. The NHATS rounds are a year
apart, which is coarse beside the weeks over which recovery from an acute
illness takes place, and the thesis uses a single baseline round. The
dynamical extension of Appendix~\ref{app:dynamical} shows where a measure of
resilience would sit: in a model with an equilibrium, the speed of return
after a small disturbance is set by the slope of the drift at that
equilibrium, and a slope falling towards zero is the slowing of recovery that
\citet{scheffer_2009} describe. Estimating it would need frequent
measurements of the biomedical state, which this thesis does not have.

\paragraph{A threshold in the measure is not a threshold in the person.} One
point needs care. The scoring rule of Chapter~\ref{ch:methods} maps each
domain onto the unit interval using the 1st and 99th percentiles of the
development split, and places anyone below the lower bound at, in effect,
zero. At zero, the geometric form gives no weight to the other domains. That
is a threshold, but it is a property of the measuring system, not of the
person. It would be a mistake to read a score of zero as evidence that a
person has crossed a critical transition. The fixed-floor sensitivity
analysis, which maps the same bounds onto $[0.05,1]$, exists to show how much
the results depend on this artefact of measurement. The distinction between
a threshold in the world and a threshold in the instrument leads to the second
half of the chapter, which treats the instrument as a system in its own right.

\section{The Measurement Pipeline as an Engineered System}
\label{sec:sys-pipeline}

The first half of the chapter treated the system being measured. The last
section ended on a threshold that belongs to the instrument rather than to the
person, and that is the point at which the second system comes into view.
The measuring system is not a single model. It is a chain of stages, each
built by hand, each taking the output of the stage before it, and each able to
fail. Systems engineering offers a discipline for such chains: state what the
system must do as requirements, define the interfaces between its parts,
control changes to its configuration, and check both that it was built as
specified and that what was specified is what was needed
\citep{incose_2015}. This section applies that discipline to the pipeline of
this thesis.

\paragraph{Stages.} The pipeline has nine stages. It starts from the NHATS
public-use files as released: the Round~5 sample-person file for baseline
information, the follow-up files for Rounds~6 to~10, the latest tracker file
for vital status, the Round~1 file for items asked only at a first interview,
and the income imputation file. A layout check then confirms that every
variable declared in the layout file is present in the release in use, and
that every observed value is a declared code or one of the NHATS reserved
missing-data codes; the pipeline refuses to proceed while a declared variable
is absent. The build stage restricts the sample to people with a Round~5
sample-person interview, fills education and gender from Round~1 for
continuing members, codes the eleven indicators and dates each outcome in
months from the Round~5 interview. The imputation stage fills missing
indicators by chained equations in the development split, using the
indicators, age, sex and four baseline flags but no outcome. The measurement
stage fits the joint three-domain factor model to each imputed set. The
scoring stage averages each person's factor scores over the imputations and
maps them onto the unit interval with bounds taken from the development split,
then applies the realisation function and computes the comparator scores. The
survival stage fits the Cox models, with cause-specific models for
hospitalisation and death before hospitalisation. The validation stage
measures discrimination, calibration and net benefit, refitting the whole
chain in each bootstrap resample and reporting results by subgroup. The export
stage writes tables and figures into the thesis from the analysis outputs,
with counts below 11 suppressed. Chapter~\ref{ch:methods} specifies each stage
in full; the point here is their arrangement.

\paragraph{The stages in laboratory terms.} Several of these stages have a
direct counterpart in laboratory practice, and stating them may help a reader
from the laboratory follow the rest of the chapter. A factor model estimates a
quantity that cannot be observed directly, here each domain, from several
indicators that each reflect it in part. An analyte concentration is inferred
from an assay signal through a calibration in the same way, and a factor
loading, the weight that links an indicator to its domain, says how strongly
that indicator tracks the domain, as a sensitive assay tracks its analyte.
Multiple imputation by chained equations fills each missing indicator by
predicting it from the others, and repeats the fill several times so that the
uncertainty of the fill is carried into the results; a laboratory has no exact
counterpart, since it repeats a measurement rather than predicting one. The
survival stage uses Cox models, familiar from cohort studies, with people lost
to follow-up treated as censored. Its cause-specific models handle competing
risks: a death before any admission prevents that admission from being seen,
as the death of a trial participant prevents the later event of interest from
being recorded. In the validation stage, discrimination is what the area under
an ROC curve measures for a diagnostic test, the ability to rank people who
have the event above people who do not. Calibration is agreement between
predicted and observed rates, checked across the range of risk as an assay is
checked against standards of known concentration. Net benefit asks whether
acting on the score at a chosen threshold of risk does more good than harm,
which is the question weighed in setting a diagnostic cut-off that trades
false positives against missed cases. The bootstrap repeats the whole
validation on resampled data, as a validation run might be repeated on fresh
material drawn from the same pool, and measurement invariance asks whether the
score reads the same for the same underlying capacity in different groups, as
an assay should give the same reading for the same concentration in different
patient groups or laboratories.

\paragraph{Requirements.} A requirement is a statement of what the system
must do, written so that it can be checked. The pipeline's requirements come
from two sources. Some follow from the theory: that outcomes never enter
measurement, because a construct fitted to the outcomes it later predicts is a
risk score and not a construct. Some follow from the data: that every variable
name and code matches what NHATS documents, because a pipeline that reads a
valid code with the wrong meaning will run without complaint and produce wrong
results. Others follow from the duty to readers and to participants: that every
printed number can be traced to code that produced it, and that no table can
identify a small group. Table~\ref{tab:sys-requirements} lists eight of them,
with the place in the thesis that states each and the check that tests it.

\paragraph{Interfaces.} The stages meet at interfaces, and most failures in
engineered systems occur there, where one part assumes something about
another that is not true. The pipeline has four interfaces that matter. The
first lies between the NHATS documents and the code, and its form is the
layout file: one file that declares every variable name and response code the
build uses, with the source document for each. The second lies between the
raw files and the analysis, and its form is a data contract: the analysis
expects named columns of stated types, unique identifiers, valid weights and
event times, and the contract check refuses data that do not meet it. The
third lies between measurement and prediction, and its form is the frozen
measurement model. The survival stage reads the loadings, imputation models
and scaling bounds; it cannot write to them. That one-way interface is the
engineering form of Assumption~\ref{ass:outcome_blind}. The fourth lies
between the analysis outputs and the thesis, and its form is the export
stage, which writes tables from outputs so that no number is copied by hand.

\paragraph{Configuration control.} A system whose parts can be changed one at
a time needs a record of which version of each part produced which result.
The pipeline keeps that record in three ways. There is one layout file, so a
name or code is declared in one place and changed in one place. Every change
to the code and the layout is held under version control, so any result can
be traced to the state of the files that produced it. And the study
configuration, which fixes the indicator set and directions, the weight
$\alpha$, the range of the substitution parameter $\rho$, the scaling bounds,
the comparators, the subgroups, the horizons and the rejection rules, is
to be frozen and committed before any outcome is opened. In engineering terms that
commitment is a requirement freeze. It does not prevent change. It makes any
later change visible as a change made after the outcomes were seen, which is
the information a reader needs to judge it.

\paragraph{Verification and validation.} The distinction between these two
checks is the most useful single idea that systems engineering brings to this
thesis. Verification asks whether the system computes what was specified.
Validation asks whether what was specified does what it was meant to do
\citep{incose_2015}. A pipeline can pass verification and fail validation: it
can compute exactly the factor model, the scores and the survival models it
was designed to compute, and the construct can still fail to measure anything
distinct or to predict anything. The reverse cannot happen in any useful sense.
If the pipeline fails verification, its validation results describe an object
other than the one specified, and they cannot be read as evidence about the
construct.

A laboratory draws the same line when it brings in a new analyser or method.
It verifies that the analyser reproduces the manufacturer's specification for
precision, accuracy and linearity in its own hands, and it validates that the
method is fit for the clinical use intended, for instance that its reference
range and decision limits suit the patients it will serve. An analyser can
pass verification and still be unsuitable for a given clinical question. The
parallel has one limit. A laboratory verifies against a specification that a
manufacturer established and documented independently. Here the author of the
pipeline also wrote its specification, so the checks against the NHATS
documents in Appendix~\ref{app:datachecks}, which come from outside the
thesis, carry part of the weight that a manufacturer's documentation carries
in the laboratory.

The thesis keeps the two apart. Verification is carried by two appendices.
Appendix~\ref{app:verification} records a suite in which each numbered result
that can be checked by computation is asserted by a test named for it, and the
table of outcomes is regenerated with every build, so it cannot report a state
of affairs that has ceased to be true. Appendix~\ref{app:datachecks} records
the check of every variable name and code against the NHATS documents, and the
tests that keep each correction in place. Validation is carried by the
empirical chapters. Chapter~\ref{ch:results1} asks whether the measurement
model fits, whether the domains are distinct and reliable, and whether the
structure holds across groups. Chapter~\ref{ch:results2} asks whether the
score predicts death and hospitalisation, whether it is calibrated and whether
it adds to established measures. Verification comes first in the order of
work because a validation result on an unverified pipeline has no clear
meaning.

Two limits of verification should be stated. A test suite tests what its
author thought to test; Appendix~\ref{app:verification} says this of its own
suite, and records two errors that were caught only because a test happened
to assert the right property. And verification against the NHATS documents
can confirm a name and a code but not that the code means, in every case,
what the documents say. Both limits lead to the next section, which asks how
the pipeline can fail and what stands in the way of each failure.

\section{Failure Modes and Their Effects}
\label{sec:sys-fmea}

Requirements say what the pipeline must do. They do not say how it can fail,
and a list of requirements that are each met can still describe a system that
fails in a way no requirement anticipated. Failure mode and effects analysis
approaches the question from the other end \citep{stamatis_2003}. For each
part of a system it asks how the part could fail, what the failure would do
to the rest of the system, how it would be detected, and what control
prevents it or limits its effect. In industrial practice each failure mode is
also scored for severity, frequency and ease of detection, and the scores are
multiplied to rank them. The thesis does not score them: there is no record of
how often each failure occurs in pipelines of this kind, and numbers made up to
fill the columns would give a false impression of precision. It lists them
instead.

The method will be familiar from laboratory risk management, where quality
systems apply failure mode and effects analysis to the steps of the testing
process, from the request and the collection of a sample to the release of a
report. The pipeline can be read in the same three phases. The build stages,
which assemble and code the data, play the part of the pre-analytical phase;
the measurement, scoring and survival stages are the analytical phase; and the
export stage, which writes the numbers that readers see, is the
post-analytical phase. The five errors actually found here all sat in the
first phase, before any model was fitted.

Table~\ref{tab:sys-fmea} sets out ten failure modes. The first five are real.
Each is an error that was present in the pipeline and was caught by the checks
of Appendix~\ref{app:datachecks} before any analysis of NHATS data. The last

\paragraph{What the real failures share.} The five errors in the upper half of
Table~\ref{tab:sys-fmea} have one feature in common, and it is the reason they
are instructive. None of them would have stopped the pipeline. Each involved a
valid code read with the wrong meaning, a value missing for a reason the code
did not know, or a status the code treated as something it was not. The
automated check of presence and code range passes all of them, because the
codes were all codes NHATS uses. They were found by a slower check: reading,
item by item, what NHATS says each code means and to whom each question was
put. The lesson for design is that an automated check confirms the form of the
data and a documentary check confirms its meaning, and a pipeline needs both.

Their effects also differ in kind. The reversed activity codes would have
inverted part of an indicator and produced a wrong measurement. The missing
education values would have replaced an observed indicator with an imputed one
for a large part of the cohort, which is a quieter error: the results would
have looked plausible and been less informative than they appeared. The
remaining three concern who is followed and for how long. Decedents carrying a
weight would have entered a cohort meant to start alive. The missed death code
and the untraced rounds would have biased follow-up in one direction, by
treating deaths as losses or by extending the time at risk past the point at
which the person was known to be alive; both would have shifted estimated risk
downwards. None of the five would have been visible in the results.

\paragraph{Common-cause failure.} A common-cause failure is one in which a
single fault disables several parts at once, and it is dangerous because it
defeats checks that rely on comparing those parts. The layout file is a
common cause by design. It feeds every indicator, every comparator and every
outcome, so an error in it can reach many places at once. The activity code
error shows the danger concretely: it touched the function indicator and two
of the comparators (Table~\ref{tab:G_changes}). The thesis compares the
H-potential score with those comparators to judge whether it adds anything.
If both the score and a comparator draw on the same misread items, the
comparison cannot reveal the error, because both are wrong in the same
direction. The comparison of construct and comparator is therefore not an
independent check of the data layer. The defence against a common cause has
to sit at the common cause itself, which is why the layout file cites a source
for every entry and why each correction has its own test.

A laboratory meets the same problem with a calibrator. One faulty calibrator
affects every result run on it, across every patient and every analyte it
calibrates, and comparing two results that share it will not show the fault,
because both carry the same bias. The fault is found only by a check that does
not share the calibrator, such as control material whose values were assigned
independently. The layout file is the calibrator of this pipeline, and the
documentary check against NHATS is the independent material.

There is a reason the layout file is a single point nonetheless. The
alternative, declaring names and codes in each script that uses them, would
remove the common cause and replace it with many separate chances to make the
same mistake differently in each place. One file that is checked against the
documents is easier to get right than many that are not. The cost of the
choice is that an error in it travels far, and the control is to check it
hardest.

\paragraph{Defence in depth.} \citet{reason_1990} argued that failures in
complex systems seldom have a single cause. They arise when latent
conditions, flaws built into the system long before, combine with an active
error or an unusual circumstance, and when each of the defences between them
happens to have a gap in the same place. The remedy is several independent
layers of defence, so that an error which passes one is stopped by another.
The pipeline has such layers. The documentary check of
Appendix~\ref{app:datachecks} comes first and catches errors of meaning. The
pre-build check catches absent variables and unexpected codes, including those
that appear when NHATS reissues a file. The regression tests catch a
correction that a later edit undoes. The verification suite of
Appendix~\ref{app:verification} catches a formula that does not compute what
the text states. The bootstrap and the sensitivity analyses catch results that
depend on chance or on a single arbitrary choice. Internal validation by
subgroup catches a model that is right on average and wrong for some. The
governance rule on use catches, or at least forbids, the misuse that no part
of the pipeline can see. The latent conditions in Reason's sense are the
errors in the layout that sit unnoticed until data run through them; the five
caught here were latent conditions of exactly that kind.

Laboratories build their quality systems on the same principle. Internal
quality control runs control material with every batch and catches drift or
error within the laboratory's own runs. External quality assessment sends the
same samples to many laboratories and catches a bias that the laboratory's own
controls share and so cannot see. Neither layer replaces the other. In the
pipeline, the regression tests, the pre-build check and the verification suite
play the part of internal quality control. The nearest counterpart to external
quality assessment would be an independent group rebuilding the analytic file
from the NHATS public-use files and comparing results, and the thesis does not
report such a comparison. The documentary check against NHATS is external in
its reference, but it was carried out within this study, so it lacks the
independence of a scheme that compares many laboratories.

\paragraph{Tight coupling.} \citet{perrow_1984} argued that some systems will
fail however carefully they are run, because their parts are tightly coupled,
so that a failure in one passes quickly to the next with no slack and no
chance to intervene, and their interactions are complex enough that operators
cannot foresee how two failures will combine. A scripted pipeline is tightly
coupled. Each stage consumes the last one's output at once and without
inspection, and the speed and repeatability that make scripting valuable are
the same properties that let an error pass from the first stage to the last
in seconds. The pipeline loosens its coupling at chosen points. It stops when
a declared variable is absent rather than proceeding with what it has. It
reports every tracker status value and every value outside the declared codes,
so that a person reads them before the build continues. It records which
income source it used. Each of these is a point at which a human can see what
the machine is about to do. The cost is that the pipeline is slower to run and
more verbose than it need be. The benefit is that a person reads what the
machine would otherwise pass over in silence, and every status value the
tracker holds is now listed for that reading.

Perrow's argument has a second edge. Adding defences adds parts, and the
defences can themselves fail or interact. A test suite that is wrong can give
false comfort, as Appendix~\ref{app:verification} records of a conservation
test that passed while a sign error moved probability the wrong way. The
layers of defence above are therefore not a guarantee. They reduce the chance
that an error of a known kind reaches the results. They say nothing about
errors of a kind not yet imagined.

The failure modes so far end at the edge of the pipeline. The last row of
Table~\ref{tab:sys-fmea} does not: misuse of the score happens after the
pipeline has done its work. The next section treats the people who use the
score as part of the system.

\section{Who Acts on the Score: the Control Structure}
\label{sec:sys-control}

The pipeline produces a number. What happens next depends on who reads it and
what they do. \citet{leveson_2011} argued that safety in complex systems is
best understood as a problem of control rather than of component failure.
A system is safe when every controller, human or automatic, has an adequate
model of the process it controls, issues control actions that keep the
process within safe limits, and receives feedback that tells it what its
actions have done. Accidents arise when a controller's model of the process
drifts from the process itself, so that actions that seemed right turn out to
be wrong. In Leveson's terms a score is not a controller. It is part of the
feedback that controllers receive, and a change in that feedback changes what
they do.

\paragraph{Controllers.} Three kinds of controller could act on an
H-potential score if it were used, and the thesis treats such use as outside
the present validation. A clinician acting on one person could use the domain
profile to decide whom to refer, what to review and which barrier to address
in a care plan. A programme manager acting on a caseload could use scores to
decide whom to reach first, or which service to offer to whom. A policy maker
acting on a population could use the distribution of domain scores to decide
where to spend a budget and which eligibility rules to set. Above all three
sit the researchers who maintain the model and decide when it must be
recalibrated.

Figure~\ref{fig:sys-control} draws this control structure. The population
and the care system form the controlled process at the bottom. The three
controllers above it act on the process through the control actions marked on
the downward arrows, and learn what their actions did through the feedback on
the upward arrows, which carries the score, the domain profile and observed
outcomes. The model maintainers sit above the controllers: they set the rules
for using the score and recalibrate it, and they learn whether the rules hold
through monitoring of the process, which reaches them only after years. The
line style of each feedback path marks its delay.

\paragraph{Control actions and feedback.} Each controller receives feedback
on a different time scale. A clinician sees one person again within weeks or
months and learns quickly whether a referral helped, but learns about one
person at a time. A programme manager sees caseload outcomes over months, with
the delays of reporting and the noise of small numbers. A policy maker sees
population outcomes over years, often through surveys such as NHATS itself,
which report a year or more after the events they describe. The longer the
feedback delay, the longer a controller's model of the process can drift
before anything corrects it, and the delays of Section~\ref{sec:sys-stocks}
add to the delays of reporting. A policy that acts on Systemic Burdens may not
show its effect on capacity for years, and by then the policy maker may have
moved on.

\paragraph{Unsafe control actions.} Leveson classifies unsafe control actions
into four kinds: an action needed for safety is not given; an unsafe action is
given; an action is given too early, too late or in the wrong order; or an
action is stopped too soon or continued too long. Each has a counterpart in
the use of this score. The clearest unsafe action is to use the score to
withhold care, for example by judging that a person with low realised
capability will gain too little from treatment to justify it. The realisation
function makes this temptation concrete, since the gain from a biomedical
intervention is smaller where context is poor. Reading that as a reason to
deny care reverses the purpose of the construct, which is to show where
context should be improved. A needed action can be omitted when a moderate
summary score hides a domain near the floor, which is why the domain profile
must be reported with the score. An action can come too late when the score
is updated only at long intervals. And a model can be used too long, after the
population it was calibrated in has changed, or in a population it was never
calibrated in, which is the problem of transport that Chapter~\ref{ch:methods}
treats as a fresh validation rather than a calculation.

\paragraph{Allocation feedback and prevention.} \citet{bishai_2014} used a
systems model to argue that health systems tend to under-invest in prevention
because the visible demand of people who are already ill draws resources
towards cure, and the resulting shortfall in prevention adds to the number of
people who need cure. A score used to allocate services could repeat that
pattern. If resources follow the lowest scores, they go to people whose
domains have already fallen far, and not to people whose domains are
intermediate and falling, for whom a timely change might prevent the fall. The
domain profile offers a different use: to find the binding domain while it is
still above the floor, when the reinforcing loops of
Figure~\ref{fig:sys-loops} have not yet taken hold. Whether any such use does
good is an empirical question about programmes, which the thesis does not
address. The point here is that the allocation rule is part of the system, and
a rule that seems neutral can set up a feedback of its own.

\paragraph{A score in use changes what it measures.} The last point is the
one that most separates a deployed score from a research finding. A model of
risk describes a system in which the model is not used. Once it is used, it
becomes part of the system. If programmes direct help to people with low
scores and the help works, the relation between a low score and a poor
outcome weakens, and the model will appear to lose its accuracy precisely
because it has succeeded. If low scores lead to care being withheld, the
relation strengthens, and the model will appear more accurate than it is
because it has caused some of the outcomes it predicts. Indicators can also
change meaning once they matter: an item that was answered without thought
in a survey may be answered differently when the answer affects access to a
service. In each case the validation reported in this thesis describes the
system before the score entered it.

It follows that monitoring after deployment is a requirement of any use, not
an optional extra. The monitoring would need to track calibration over time
and by subgroup, the distribution of indicator responses, and the outcomes of
people who were acted on and of people who were not. It would also need a
route by which a person can learn why they were scored as they were and
challenge it. Chapter~\ref{ch:methods} states that person-level use requires an
explanation and appeal mechanism and that the score may not be the sole basis
for automated denial, reimbursement or triage. In Leveson's terms these are
constraints placed on the controllers, and the monitoring is the feedback that
tells the maintainers whether the constraints hold.

Laboratory practice offers a close parallel. Validating a method once does not
end the laboratory's duty to it: quality control continues for as long as the
method is in use, because reagents, instruments and patient populations
change. A score in use needs the same continuing check, shown as the
monitoring path in Figure~\ref{fig:sys-control}. The parallel is weaker in one
respect. A control sample does not change because the laboratory acted on the
last result, while a population whose care was changed by a score is no longer
the population in which the score was validated.

\section{The Limits of Systems Thinking}
\label{sec:sys-limits}

The chapter has used systems ideas to fix a boundary, describe feedback, rank
places to intervene, separate reserve from resilience, set requirements and
list failure modes. A reader may fairly ask what this has shown. The answer
is less than the vocabulary might suggest, and this section says why.

\citet{box_1976} observed that since every model is wrong, the scientist
cannot reach a correct one by elaboration, and should instead seek an
economical description and worry about what is importantly wrong. The loop
diagram of Figure~\ref{fig:sys-loops} is a model in this sense, and a weak
one. It names variables and the signs of the links between them. It gives no
strengths, no delays in months and no functional forms. A diagram of that
kind predicts nothing on its own, and a model that predicts nothing cannot be
tested. Worse, a loop diagram can explain almost any behaviour after the
fact: if a person declines, a reinforcing loop dominated; if they recover, the
balancing loop did. An account that fits every outcome is not supported by any
of them. The diagram earns its place only where it leads to a claim that could
fail, and in this thesis it leads to two: that the domains should be
correlated but distinct, and that a baseline reading should predict later
outcomes. Both are tested in the empirical chapters, and neither is unique to
the loops.

Boundary choices are judgements, as \citet{checkland_1981} insisted, and a
different judgement would produce a different system. A boundary that made
health services and policy endogenous would turn several of the givens of
Section~\ref{sec:sys-boundary} into variables, and the feedback in
Figure~\ref{fig:sys-loops} would include the response of services to the
needs they observe. The conclusions about where to act could change. The
thesis chose its boundary to match what NHATS can observe, and that is a
reason of feasibility, not of truth.

A related risk is that of linking everything to everything. In a system as
large as a person's health and circumstances, almost every factor affects
almost every other through some path. A diagram that drew all of them would
be complete and useless, since it would offer no basis for choosing one
intervention over another. The figure in this chapter is kept to three domains
and one service flow for that reason. The cost is that it leaves out paths
that may matter, and the reader should not take its sparseness as a claim that
those paths are absent.

The engineering half has limits of its own. A failure mode and effects
analysis lists the failures its author thought of; the ten in
Table~\ref{tab:sys-fmea} are not all the ways the pipeline could fail. A
requirements table describes what was required, not what should have been.
And the language of verification can lend a pipeline an authority it has not
earned, since a pipeline that does exactly what was specified is only as good
as the specification.

The use this thesis makes of systems thinking is therefore narrow. It uses it
to structure questions, by asking of each part of the construct what it
depends on and what depends on it, and to design checks, by asking of each
stage of the pipeline how it could fail and what would catch the failure. It
does not use systems thinking as evidence for the construct. Whether the three
domains exist, whether the indicators measure them and whether the score
predicts outcomes are questions for the data, and nothing in this chapter
answers them.

\section*{What Is New in This Chapter}

Systems thinking in health is not new: \citet{peters_2014},
\citet{diez_roux_2011} and \citet{rutter_2017} apply it to health systems,
disparities and interventions, and the tools used here, stocks and flows,
feedback loops, places to intervene, failure mode and effects analysis and
control structures, come from the sources cited in each section. This chapter
applies those tools to a measure of individual health capacity and to the
pipeline that computes it, and adds four things. First, it gives a feedback
account of the three domains (Figures~\ref{fig:sys-loops}
and~\ref{fig:sys-stocks}) that supplies a dynamic reason for the
multiplicative realisation function of Chapter~\ref{chap:crf}; the account is
a set of hypotheses, and Section~\ref{sec:sys-loops} names the longitudinal
study that would test it. Second, it states eight requirements of the measuring
system and sorts each check into verification or validation
(Table~\ref{tab:sys-requirements}); each verification check is either a
named test in Appendix~\ref{app:verification} or a recorded item-by-item check
in Appendix~\ref{app:datachecks}, so a reader can confirm it by running the
tests or repeating the check. Third, it lists the failure modes of the pipeline
(Table~\ref{tab:sys-fmea}), five of which are errors actually found and
corrected before analysis, each now guarded by a test. Fourth, it sets out who
would act on the score and with what delays (Figure~\ref{fig:sys-control}),
from which it derives the requirement that any use be monitored after
deployment; that requirement can be checked in any deployment by asking
whether calibration is tracked over time and by subgroup.

\section{What This Chapter Settles}
\label{sec:sys-close}

The chapter began by asking what changes when the construct and the
instrument that measures it are treated as systems. It settles four things.
It fixes the boundary of the measured system at the person, the household and
the part of services the person meets, with NHATS as the interface through
which the system is seen. It reads capacity as a stock and realised health as
a flow, which explains why a single reading can carry information about later
outcomes and why it cannot measure recovery. It shows that a multiplicative
realisation function makes the weakest domain the one where a unit of change
does most, so that improving a strong domain buys little. And it sets out the
pipeline as a chain of stages with stated requirements, named interfaces,
listed failure modes and layered defences, in which verification and
validation are kept apart.

It leaves open the questions that only data can answer: whether the feedback
of Figure~\ref{fig:sys-loops} operates, whether the three domains can be
measured as distinct, and whether the score predicts what the theory says it
should. Each later chapter takes something from this one.

Chapter~\ref{ch:methods}, the formalism, takes the boundary and the
observation interface, which set what the analysis can and cannot know, and
the one-way interface between measurement and prediction, which it states as
Assumption~\ref{ass:outcome_blind}. Its account of the analytic file, the
timing of events and the scoring rule is the specification against which the
pipeline is verified.

Chapter~\ref{chap:crf}, the realisation model, takes the reading of the
realisation function as an interface that couples the domains in series. Its
axioms state formally what Section~\ref{sec:sys-boundary} stated in words,
its cross-partials are the static trace of the compounding that
Section~\ref{sec:sys-loops} describes, and its policy gradient is the formal
version of the argument of Section~\ref{sec:sys-leverage} that the binding
domain is where a unit of change does most.

Chapter~\ref{ch:results1}, on the measurement model, tests whether the
components of Section~\ref{sec:sys-boundary} are distinct. Correlated domains
would be consistent with the feedback of Section~\ref{sec:sys-loops}, and also
with common causes, and the chapter does not claim to separate them. Its
invariance tests are the validation of the last requirement in
Table~\ref{tab:sys-requirements}.

Chapter~\ref{ch:results2}, on prognostic performance, tests the persistence
of the stocks that Section~\ref{sec:sys-stocks} identified as the basis for
prediction, and applies the controls against optimism and subgroup
miscalibration listed in Table~\ref{tab:sys-fmea}.

Chapter~\ref{ch:translational}, the haemophilia application, redraws the
boundary for one disease. It substitutes disease-specific indicators and
treats the move as a new validation problem, as Section~\ref{sec:sys-control}
says any change of population must be. Its dynamical companion in the
appendices makes one balancing loop explicit, in which treatment restores a
factor level that disease and clearance draw down.

Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} returns to the limits of
Section~\ref{sec:sys-limits} and to the control structure of
Section~\ref{sec:sys-control}, asking what the results permit and what any
use of the score would require. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} states what the
thesis settles.

The next step is to make the measuring system precise. This chapter has said
what the pipeline must do and how it can fail. Chapter~\ref{ch:methods} says
exactly what it computes: the data, the factor structures, the score and the
survival models, each specified before any outcome is seen, so that the
verification described here has a specification to verify against.
